Operations design

Hardware Strength & Conditioning

I built the programming pipeline for a local community gym. Three months of training gets written, the coach makes it theirs, it lands in the athlete app, and the emails and Instagram captions come along for the ride. Mostly it gave a coach their Sundays back.

How it holds together

The design work was deciding where authority sits. The moment a month lands in the sheet, the sheet wins. The coach edits workouts and notes right there, and nothing regenerates over a hand-edit. Entry reads from the sheet instead of the conversation, so a change made on Tuesday is simply what gets entered. That one rule is what keeps a generated artifact from quietly overwriting a human decision.

The rest is friction I removed by actually running it. A template tab that duplicates instead of a schema that rebuilds. One bulk paste instead of cell by cell writes. Movement names pinned to the platform's own vocabulary so its parser stops swapping in the wrong lift. Anything a run teaches goes back into the prompts, so the system gets cheaper to operate instead of more elaborate.

It also shipped, which is the part I care about. I set the coach up with Claude and taught them Claude Code, so this runs without me. Designing something a non engineer can actually operate is a different problem from designing something that works. It's why the prompts read as plain instructions, why setup is "duplicate this tab", and why every step is something a coach can run on a Sunday without debugging anything.

Built for one gym and structured to move. Every gym specific detail lives in one standing context file, so taking this somewhere else means swapping a single document.

Read the pipeline as text
  1. Duplicate the TEMPLATE tab for the new month. No schema to rebuild.
  2. Generate the month. The coach chooses Monday, Tuesday, and Friday strength, and the rest is written to match, entry ready.
  3. One TSV paste into the first data cell. The whole month lands in a single action.
  4. The sheet becomes the source of truth. The coach edits workouts and notes directly. Nothing regenerates over a hand-edit.
  5. Enter into btwb, reading from the sheet, so any sheet edit carries through.
  6. Comms. Cycle launch email, plus a weekly Instagram caption and coaching meeting summary.
  7. Fold anything learned back into the prompts, so the next run starts cleaner.

Analytics & BI

Dashboards and reporting

My professional BI work is confidential, so it can't live here. The tools and the thinking can.

How I approach it

Most of what makes a dashboard useful happens before anything gets built. Which question it answers, who is actually going to open it, and what decision changes based on what they see. A chart that nobody acts on is decoration.

The same care applies to how the numbers get drawn. Axes that start where they should, time frames that don't flatter, and a real trend line instead of a wall of figures. How data gets represented shapes how decisions get made, so the representation is part of the analysis and not a step that comes after it.

Team project

UX Navigator

A guide to the double diamond design process, built for the person running the process before they have the words for it. Start at any phase, and the templates for it are right there.

The architecture decision

We decided not to lean on search. Assuming every visitor already knows the vocabulary would have failed the people who needed the resource most. So the site walks you through one phase at a time while still letting you jump sideways between phase specific resources and templates, the way the design process actually behaves rather than the way it diagrams.

We built it around a persona whose real problem was time to resource, meaning how long it took to find the right thing at any point in a project. Next steps we already know about are collapsible responsive navigation and building out the community layer.

Design challenge

Greyhounds Oasis

A redesign for a greyhound rescue, built mobile first around the only thing that really matters, getting a dog home.

What I changed

Rescue sites tend to bury the adoption path under history, policy, and donation asks. I pulled Adopt up to where you land, gave the navigation a clearer structure, and made room for a Hound Highlight so real adoptions are visible instead of abstract.

Seeing a specific dog with a specific family does more work than any amount of copy about the mission. This one is close to home for me. I foster and I love these dogs, so I already knew what the adopter side of that page feels like.

Design challenge

FCC

A government site rebuilt so people can find the thing they actually came for.

What I changed

Federal sites are usually organized around how the agency is structured rather than why anyone visits. Most people arrive with a complaint to file or a program to check eligibility for, so those got surfaced as Quick Links instead of sitting three levels down.

The rest was making the language and the layout feel like they were written for the public, since the audience here is everyone.
